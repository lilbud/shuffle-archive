You wouldn’t be reading these words if you weren’t a hardcore Springsteen fan.

Like me, you know every song the man ever recorded and released. We may share a common fascination with alternate versions, outtakes, and early versions that only exist on bootlegs.

We could probably go head to head in a “Name That Show” contest when a live track airs on E Street Radio.

But I have to admit: if it weren’t for my blog, there’s no way I would have given more than a passing glance to the majority of songs in this book. At least some of them may be new-ish to you, too.

But once you start on an obsessive quest to write about every composition an artist ever created, where do you draw the line? Apparently, somewhere other than in his teenage notebook.

So here we are.

This *Roll of the Dice* installment isn’t like any of the others. It’s not even comparable to Volume Eight (The Bruce Springsteen Band), because at least that one had audio for every song.

This book, it’s safe to say, truly covers Springsteen’s earliest formative years as a musician–to the point where sometimes we’ll be analyzing handwriting because that’s all we’ve been given to work with.

The entries that follow are organized roughly chronologically. We start with four songs from Bruce’s earliest band, The Castiles, including his first two studio recordings ever.

From there, we rifle through a notebook of song lyrics he wrote at the age of eighteen. These sixteen songs comprise the heart of the book.

The notebook entries continue on from here, overlapping with Bruce’s Steel Mill period, but we’ll save the songs he wrote for the band for a future *Roll of the Dice* volume.

Four songs follow that were only ever performed by the short-lived Dr. Zoom and The Sonic Boom. One could argue that these songs could and should have been included in the Bruce Springsteen Band installment (the band line-ups were essentially the same), and I would not argue back. But here they are anyway, complete with audio.

Finally, we close with a pair of songs recorded right on the cusp of Bruce’s first album. The first is “Family Song,” an outtake that honestly should have been included in Volume One. That was my omission, and this is my attempt to make amends.

“Water Station” is the second one, and it’s tough to classify. When I wrote about it, I called it a piece of ephemera, and that’s as good a description as any: an improvised but original composition conjured at the same time it was being performed, just a few months before Springsteen’s first album would reach record stores.

If you’ve never heard it, you’re not alone. Until I shared it on my blog (with the permission of an anonymous uber collector), it had never circulated in fan circles.

So enjoy this time travel trip to the prehistoric Springsteen era. We’ll return to the official catalog in the next edition.
